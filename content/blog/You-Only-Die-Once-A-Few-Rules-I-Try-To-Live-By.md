I keep a list. Not a formal one, just notes in a file that I add to when something sticks. Half of it I wrote down years ago and would defend today. The other half I've already changed my mind about, which is the point of writing them down at all.

Some of these I picked up from somewhere years ago and never managed to track down where. I'm not claiming I came up with them. This is my understanding of them in my own words, and I stand by it.

These are the ones that survived.

## You Only Die Once

People say you only live once. They mean it kindly, but it's wrong. You die once. You live every day, and most days don't feel like anything while you're in them.

You get up, drink water, make small choices that don't register, do it again. Then one day you notice you haven't done anything you actually wanted for eleven years, and you can't work out when that happened.

So live the day. Not the year. Not the plan for the year. Today, and whatever today actually contains.

## Two Opposites, Both True

You can build a whole life on two opposite principles at the same time. Picking one and building on it is what doesn't work.

Two examples I live with.

**Be careful, and commit.** Research first, refuse to rush, look before you decide. Also decide, commit hard, and stop reopening it every third day. Careful turns into cowardice when you use it as somewhere to hide. Decisiveness turns into recklessness when you use it to avoid thinking. Both in you at once, and the skill is knowing which one this moment needs.

**Give freely, and protect yourself.** Hand over money to family, mean it completely, never mention it again. Also don't let people take from you repeatedly without consequence. If you only give, people walk over you until you resent them, and you weren't free then, you were just afraid to say no. If you only protect, you end up alone, which isn't safety, just a slower version of the same result.

You don't resolve the tension by choosing. You hold both and make the call fresh each time.

## The 37-63 Rule

There's a math problem about this. When you have to pick one thing and can't go back, spend the first 37% of your window just looking. Learn what the options look like. Don't commit. Then commit to the first thing that beats everything you saw in that first stretch.

It works for people, jobs, apartments, cities. Mostly it works because it forces you to admit that choosing is the actual event. Not the safe part. The part where options close behind you.

Both failure modes come from dodging that. Committing to the first thing because it arrived first. Or never committing and calling it thoroughness.

## Stop Arguing With What Already Happened

Ask yourself what if you relived this exact life, over and over, forever, and nothing new ever got possible. Would you curse it? Or would you shout again, again?

That question broke something in my thinking. Most of what I called suffering was me re-litigating a closed door. The meeting went badly. The trip got cancelled. The thing I wanted didn't happen. I can't un-happen any of it, and I'd keep reopening the case as if the courtroom might reconvene.

Doesn't mean pretending the bad parts were secretly good. Sometimes things just hurt. It means this is the life you get, and the only question left is whether you're going to be at war with it.

## Demand Meaning Anyway

We want meaning the way we want air. Not as a preference. As a condition of staying alive.

And the world doesn't hand any over. It's indifferent, it's quiet, and it's going to kill you eventually for reasons you can't argue with.

That collision doesn't resolve. You can't reason your way out and there's no missing manual. So push back anyway. Demand meaning even when you've got no evidence it exists. That's the whole move.

Denial says it'll be fine. Revolt says it won't be fine, I'm living anyway, and I'm going to be furious and happy about it in the same breath. The second one is honest.

## Death Is The Key, Mostly

Everyone avoids the topic and pretends it's morbid. It's the opposite. Facing the fact that this ends is what makes the rest count.

Day to day, most of us are on autopilot, doing what's expected because it's expected. That's comfortable and it's also a way of not thinking about death at all. We talk about it in the abstract, as something that happens to other people.

The mood that breaks through is anxiety. Not fear of a specific thing, just fear with no object. It tells you something true.

Where I disagree with the philosophers: they say facing death cuts you off from everyone. Doesn't match what I've seen. Death shows up in my life almost entirely through other people's deaths. My own ending is abstract. The deaths of people I love are not.

So keep the conclusion, drop the premise. Facing the end makes you real. You get there through the people who made the finite part worth having in the first place.

## Meaning In Suffering

One psychiatrist got a brutal field test for his theory. He was 37 when they sent him to a concentration camp, and he'd already built the theory.
Meaning comes from three directions: what you make or do, what you feel or experience, and how you hold suffering you can't avoid. That third one is the hardest and it's the useful one. Even when nothing can be changed, your stance is still yours.

In the camps he noticed prisoners who had something to be, a person waiting, work to finish, a belief, a task. Those survived more often.

He didn't turn it into a heartwarming story either. Suffering isn't noble, and he had no interest in making it sound noble. Accepting suffering that was avoidable is just self-harm. Meaning comes from refusing to waste the pain you had no choice about.

His line stays with me. Suffering stops being suffering the moment it finds meaning. It doesn't hurt less. It turns into something.

People say life has no purpose. Sometimes it doesn't offer one. But purpose isn't the only way to carry meaning. You might not get an answer and still get to choose how you stand while you wait.

## Poor vs. Rich

Not about money.

One of them learned technical things. How systems work, how to fix something, how to build something. The other learned how to get people to do what they want. How to read a room. When to push, when to shut up, how to tell a no from a not like this.

Both are real skills and both pay. One compounds quietly and one compounds loudly.

Reading a room isn't manipulation. It's attention. Noticing someone went quiet an hour ago and that the quiet means something. That's empathy, and it works like a muscle.

You can be broke and rich in every way that counts. You can also be wealthy and completely empty, which is its own kind of pain. Access to everything, capacity for none of it.

## Don't Treat Help As Debt

When someone better off than you offers to help, that's a choice they made. It's not an obligation they've signed up for.

The reason is usually this. They remember needing someone and having nobody. They know what it's like to be the person nobody shows up for. Now they see you in that spot and they're not being generous from some surplus of goodness. They're looking at a version of themselves that got unlucky.
They're helping their past self. You're the delivery mechanism.

This changes how you take it. If you think you're owed, you get resentful when it stops, because it was a loan and loans get defaulted on. Know that it was a gift given because someone once needed it and you can't resent that. No contract existed.

Then do the same for someone. You'll be in that position again.

## Reply Kindness, Believe In Karma Anyway

Reply to kindness with kindness. Even when it's easier not to. Especially when it costs you something.

Believe in karma, and be specific about which kind. Not the transactional version where being nice buys you a reward. The version where you get to die with a lighter heart.

The transactional version has a bug in it. It assumes you're owed. So you keep a ledger, you wonder whether it was worth it, whether it came back around, and you're disappointed when it doesn't, because you were running the numbers on your own generosity like an investment.

The other version: you do the thing, and being the person who did it is the payoff.

You might go your whole life and never see good come back from any of it. If your kindness depends on returns, that day breaks something you can't repair. Be kind when nothing comes of it. Especially then.

## Money Is Charity, Not A Loan

When you give money to friends or family, give it as charity. Assume from the moment it leaves your hand that it's gone.

They won't pay it back. Not out of malice. Most people aren't thieves, they're broke, or ashamed, or both. But they won't pay it back, and if you called it a loan, you've planted something in the relationship. It sits there, unsaid, until one of you gets angry about money and the whole thing collapses.

A gift has no residue. It's finished the second it's given. No terms, no schedule, no disappointment attached, and nobody can cite it in an argument later. And you don't get to be angry at them for not returning it, because you never made it a loan. They were never supposed to pay it back. They can't fail at something you never asked for.

The part that takes discipline: you don't get to feel bad about it afterward either. You didn't do a loan that went badly. You did a charity, it was allowed to go nowhere, and that was the whole point.

Give it, say nothing about it later, never ask, don't keep it on a list.

## Lighting Someone Else's Candle

Lighting someone else's candle doesn't dim yours. Doesn't lose anything. Doesn't shine less.

The assumption people make is zero-sum. Effort spent on someone else comes out of your account. Attention given away is attention lost. Generosity is a withdrawal, so guard the balance.

It doesn't work that way. The person you helped didn't consume something that was yours. There was never a fixed amount of goodness available and nobody is handing your share to someone else because you gave yours away.

People who think it's zero-sum have usually decided they can't afford to be generous. That decision makes everything smaller. The relationship, the work, your sense of yourself. That's the actual cost. Not the candle.

## Small Habits Compound

Big changes are loud and fragile. Small ones are quiet and hard to notice working.

Ten pages a day. 3,650 pages a year. Twelve books without ever deciding to be a reader.

Twenty minutes of walking. Over a hundred hours a year, roughly the time it takes to walk the length of a country.

One sentence written a day. That's not a journal. It becomes a book.

None of them change anything on any given day, and that's the mechanism. Transformation is doing something small so consistently that it stops being a choice and turns into a person.

## Get Out Of The Comfort Zone

Fine to visit. Bad place to live.

Separate two things though. Ordinary discomfort has an object, and you can fix it. Anxiety has no object. It's standing on groundless ground with nothing to fix. Growth lives in the first category. Want to be braver, talk to a stranger. Want to be stronger, lift something heavy. Want to make things, make something bad, in public, and finish it. That discomfort is temporary, it's teaching you something, and the only way to find out what is to stop flinching.

## Do Something Unrelated To What You Like

Strange one, and I keep coming back to it.

A brain doing the same task for years starts routing around everything else. Novelty isn't decoration. It's how you stop becoming a narrow tunnel.

If you write, go somewhere new and don't bring the laptop. If you code, learn to cook something you can't make yet. If you've been chasing money, deliberately do something that will never pay you anything.

Do something with no plausible return. It's the only reliable way to find out what you want, separate from what you were trained to want.

## Cherish The People You Love

Time with family isn't wasted. It's not the thing you do between the real work. It is the work, the part that counts and nobody invoices you for.

Time with them is production. Cracking one more problem alone at 2am is avoidance wearing a disguise.

Everyone around you is temporary, and you can see it now. Not one partner, not one friendship, not one parent is permanent. Everyone who loves you is leaving, by choice or otherwise. Not morbid. Just what you see when you pay attention. So the useful question is never how do I make this last. It's why am I on my phone right now.

## Argue From Their Side First

Before you argue, ask why. What's the benefit? What do I want to be different when this is over?

Then look at what the other person is actually saying. Even when they're being unreasonable. Especially then, because that's when everyone else stops listening and you can still learn something.

Think from their side first, then yours. Order matters here. Argue only from your own position and you produce the worst possible version of your own argument, because you're arguing from the part of yourself that's already defending instead of thinking.

Do theirs first and you get a better answer. You also find the mutual ground, which is usually sitting underneath the part neither side bothered to look at. Most arguments aren't between positions. They're between people who made their position part of their identity. You can't win those by being right.

## The Flip Test

You'll meet people with double standards. Mostly about gender, but not only. A man demanding something he'd never grant a woman. A woman dismissing something she wouldn't take from a man. A rule that only ever applies downward.

Don't rush. Before deciding, put yourself in the scene and swap the gender. Same script, swapped parts. Would that read as reasonable or would everyone laugh? Do it with age. Swap who's twenty and who's fifty. Do it with money. Swap who has it.

Then see how much of your certainty survives.

The reason it works: you can't see from inside that you drew a hand which made the rule feel natural. You're not reasoning from first principles. You're reasoning from a position that arrived with assumptions you never picked.

Two warnings. Imagining their side isn't concluding they're right. You can hold a position perfectly and still decide it's wrong. And "both sides" is where people retreat when they get caught, because sometimes the pattern really is one-sided and calling it symmetric would be a lie. Use the flip to check whether a double standard is running. Then go get the facts.

## Take Your Autonomy Back

People hold your autonomy back. Usually not out of malice. Habit, or fear, or love that comes out sideways. Someone decided a long time ago what you're allowed to want and what you're capable of, and they've been enforcing it ever since.

Everyone gets a rehearsal. When you were a teenager you started taking it back from your parents. Small stupid arguments at the worst moment. Your own music, your own clothes, your own terrible opinions. Everyone called it a phase, which is the word adults use when they'd rather not think about something harder. That phase was the whole point. Your first rehearsal at being someone who decides.

Then most people stop. Not because they lost it. Because autonomy is expensive. The second you decide your own life you own it, and nobody else is available to blame when it doesn't work. Plenty of people hand the authority back and call it peace.

Take it back in pieces if that's all that's available.

Notice what you handed over. Every habit, opinion and goal you didn't choose yourself, somebody else chose. Ask: if I had to justify this out loud to a stranger, would I still pick it?

Here's the part nobody warns you about. If people have been holding your autonomy back, they haven't been asking what you want. They've been deciding. So the relationships around you were built on a version of you that never got consulted. That's uncomfortable and it's a decent argument for doing it.

Be patient with people who push back. They read your new autonomy as rejection, because for them it is. You'll be called difficult, changed, distant, and going through a phase again, at an age where that phrase is embarrassing. That's the test. If it costs you the relationship, the relationship was built on the version of you that hadn't shown up.

Worst version of this: you've been told you can't do things long enough that you stopped trying. At that point you can't tell a real limit from one that got installed in you. Worth checking, because the alternative is building a life out of permissions other people handed you.

Taking it back isn't burning everything down. It's not a tantrum, not cutting off everyone who finds you difficult, not a licence to be unpleasant on purpose. It's deciding your own life and standing behind the decisions, including the ugly ones nobody congratulates you for.

## Walk Alone

Sometimes. Not out of bitterness, not as punishment, not as a claim you're above people.

Because not even a partner is permanent. Not one. Everyone who matters is temporary, and being at peace with walking alone is the difference between lonely and unaccompanied. They feel identical and they're nothing alike.

Your mind and body are with your soul. Everything else leaves eventually. Those two don't, and they're worth getting to know. Nobody else can do that work for you.

There's a version of solitude that's loneliness with better branding. There isn't. Go take the walk and find out which one you were doing.

## Trust Your Gut

Don't chase material things. Live where it feels right and trust your gut, because it knows you better than anyone or anything ever will.

This is the one I get wrong most. My gut isn't infallible. It's lied to me. What feels right is sometimes just familiarity in nice clothes.

Still worth listening to, because the alternative is worse. Filter every impulse through what other people would approve of and you end up with a life you can't explain. Your gut is the only part of you that isn't performing.

Not a command. A preference. When options are close and nobody gets hurt either way, go with the one that lights up.

## Be Nice For Reasons Inside You

Be nice because of the thing inside you that's unwilling to be cruel. Not because it's the easy option, and not because it isn't easy.

There's an old split between being good because you want a reward, and being good because the alternative is a violation of who you are. Someone spent a lifetime on it and landed somewhere modern ears don't like: the virtuous person does the virtuous thing because it's what a good person does, not because they were offered a deal.

Kindness that needs an audience is a performance. Kindness from a genuine "I'd rather not be that person" is the real thing, and it's the only kind that holds when nobody's watching.

## Be Mysterious

Keep something back. Not secrets, not a performance, just parts of yourself you don't explain at breakfast. People lean in. Hand over the whole story unprompted and they find something else to do.

Unfinished people are magnetic. Finished ones read like a closed file.

## Money Doesn't Buy Happiness, It Buys Everything Else

Money does not buy happiness. It's said so often it's wallpaper, and it's incomplete in a way that does damage.

Money buys options. It buys the ability to leave a bad job, to say no, to take the trip, to fix the thing, to keep going when the month is tight. It buys time, and time is the actual currency of a life.

What it actually buys: health when you need it, time always, the ability to help someone without performing it as a favor, and a buffer for the day the roof leaks. Not happiness, but most of the ground happiness needs in order to be possible.

So don't chase it for meaning, because it has none. Don't pretend it doesn't matter either. Contempt for money is usually a story rich people tell themselves.

## You Only Understand How To Live Once You Die

That's the cruel joke in the arrangement.

Everyone agrees on one thing: this ends. Nobody can use that agreement. To be any good at living you'd need the perspective you only get from being finished.

Which means you can't wait until you're ready. There's no version where you work out life and then start living. You have to be wrong repeatedly and adjust.

The wise man lives as long as he ought, not as long as he can. Lifespan isn't something to maximize. It's a fixed budget, and the only decision is whether you're spending it deliberately.

The people I admire most never had the answers. They kept going while visibly not knowing what they were doing, and were honest about it.

## You Only Know What You Know Because Someone Told You

You didn't arrive at any conclusion. You were handed one.

Humbling, and freeing. You didn't work out that murder is wrong. You were taught. You didn't invent that water is wet. You were told. And since every piece of your certainty is inherited, you're allowed to question all of it. Anything you believe, somebody handed you, in a particular century, with particular interests. That's a description of every belief you hold.

There's an old problem about this that's been bugging me. A boy knows geometry. Nobody taught him. He can explain it. If knowing were something you produced, you'd expect to produce it on demand. Instead it just shows up in people who were never taught.

Be as humble as you want. Be grateful. Somebody wrote it down so you wouldn't have to start at zero.

## Sit In The Discomfort

Clear mind. Be in the situation. Resist the reflex to run, check your phone, bail, make a joke and leave. Feel the discomfort. Don't fix it, don't numb it, don't explain it away. Stay.

After enough time it stops feeling like something you're enduring and starts feeling like something that was always there.

That's the mechanism. Everything else here is a footnote on it.

Most people never leave that spot. They get comfortable being uncomfortable. They settle for the low-grade version of themselves that only operates in situations that already feel safe. And they aren't even unhappy. They've narrowed the range of who they're willing to be until it fits inside what they've already survived.

The ones who do leave it, you can spot. Not because they announce it. Because they say things slightly too much for the room. They ask the question that makes everyone pause. They take the job that scares them. They stay at the table when leaving would be easier. You'll never hear anyone describe that as comfortable. If it feels comfortable, you already got somewhere small.

The hard part is that none of it feels like growth while it's happening. It shows up later, from a distance, when you notice you can do something you couldn't before and can't say exactly when that changed.

## The Point

There's no point. That's a discovery you have to make before you can stop looking.

What I have instead: everyone I wanted to be at twenty was never going to arrive on their own. All of them got there by being slightly wrong in public, repeatedly, over a long time, usually while nobody watched.

Nobody gets a second run. There's no patch notes. Nobody's keeping score except you. Which means, literally: nobody else is going to care what you do with it. Terrifying for about a year, then the most useful thing you'll learn.

Which leaves one question, the only one that ever mattered. What do you want to have done?
